# Funnel analysis

A funnel measures progress through a sequence of steps where some users drop off along the way. In a learning app, you might expect a session to open a topic, then use an interactive, then complete the topic. A funnel counts how many sessions reach each step, so you can see where people fall away.

In SQL, a funnel is built from an events table. Each row is an event with fields like session_id, event, and occurred_at. The counting rule is what makes it a funnel rather than three separate totals: a session counts at a step only if it fired the earlier steps first, in order. A session that completes a topic without ever opening one has not travelled the funnel, whatever its events say. Order alone is not enough, either. The steps must also happen close together, inside a time window that starts at the session's first step, or a session that opens a topic in January and completes one in June counts as a conversion nobody actually made. Distinct still matters: one session can fire the same event many times, and you are counting sessions, not raw events.

Then you calculate conversion rates. The step-to-step conversion is each step divided by the one before it: `interactive_used / topic_opened`, then `topic_completed / interactive_used`. The overall completion rate is the last step divided by the total sessions. These percentages show whether the funnel leaks early or late.

This is useful because the biggest drop between two steps is usually the best place to investigate. If many sessions open a topic but few use the interactive, the interactive might be hard to find or slow to load. If usage is high but completion is low, the content might be too long or confusing.

The SQL in the figure is the ordered version: it finds each session's first time at each step, keeps only sessions whose times rise in step order within the window, and counts those. It is longer than counting each step on its own, and that length is the price of the funnel meaning what it claims to mean.

In the figure below, the three steps are counted in order, inside the window you set. Use the window control to tighten it and watch late steps fall away: at a 1-minute window the funnel drops to 143, 59, 5, because most sessions take longer than a minute to finish a topic. Use the cohort buttons to slice the same funnel by plan or signup month; the counts and the SQL both recompute. Two switches teach the failure modes. Add the messy sessions and the figure adds five hand-written sessions whose events arrive out of order or incomplete, the way real event data actually looks. Then switch the counting to the old way, each step counted on its own, and completions jump from 45 to 50, with a warning that 2 of the counted sessions never opened a topic at all. The old way does not just round differently; it counts journeys that never happened.
