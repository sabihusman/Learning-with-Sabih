# Attention

To understand a sentence, a model has to figure out how the words relate. Read
the sentence "the animal didn't cross the street because it was tired." What does
"it" refer to? Almost certainly the animal, not the street, because streets do
not get tired. You resolved that by letting "it" look back at the other words and
deciding which one matters. Attention is how a transformer does the same thing.

## Every word looks at every other word

For each word, attention asks a simple question: to understand this word, how much
should I focus on each of the other words? The answer is a set of weights, one per
word, showing how much focus each word gets. A high weight means "this other word is
important for me right now," a low weight means "I can mostly ignore it."

Crucially, every word does this for every other word, all at once, in a single step. That is what lets a model link "it" to "animal" even though several words sit between them. Distance in the sentence does not matter; only the match between the words does. In the figure below the matches are computed, not drawn in: each word carries a small query vector and a small key vector, the figure scores every pair, and softmax turns the scores into the weights you see. The vectors here are hand-written so the pattern is visible; a real model learns its vectors from data.

## Reading the figure

The figure has two views of the same numbers. The Softmax Lab shows one word's row in full: its raw match scores against every word, the scores after scaling, and the weights softmax produces from them, with the row's total shown as 1.00. Pick "it" and its weight lands mostly on "animal" (0.41), with the next word far behind (0.08): that is the model resolving what "it" stands for, arrived at by arithmetic rather than placed there. Nudge any word's score up and watch the weights shift: the raised word gains, every other word in the row loses a little, and the total never leaves 1.00. Switch the scaling off and the weights sharpen toward the top match, which is why the scaling step exists.

The grid below the lab shows every word's row at once, with a sum column reading 1.00 on every row. Read across a row to see where that word's focus goes: "cross" leans on "street" and then "animal", and "tired" leans on "it".

A real model is not told that "it" means "animal". It learns its query, key, and value vectors from data, computes these weights on the fly for every sentence, and usually runs many attention heads at once, each head watching for a different kind of relationship.

## Why it matters

Attention is the core mechanism of the transformer, the architecture behind modern
language models. Before attention, models read text strictly in order and tended
to forget what came far earlier. Attention removed that limit: any word can draw
on any other word directly, no matter how far apart, which is what let these
models handle long passages and capture the relationships that make language mean
something.

## Query, key, and value

The mechanism you just ran in the lab is query, key, and value. Each word starts with its embedding and produces three vectors from it: a Query, representing what this word is looking for, a Key, representing what it offers to other words, and a Value, the information it passes along if another word attends to it.

To decide how much one word attends to another, the model compares the first word's Query against the second word's Key. It does this with a dot product, which is just a single number measuring how well the two vectors line up. A bigger number means a closer match.

But raw match numbers are not weights yet. Two things happen to turn them into weights. First, each match is divided by the square root of the vector size. This scaling keeps the numbers from getting too large, which would make the next step too extreme. Then all the scaled matches go through softmax, which turns a list of numbers into a set of weights that are all positive and add up to 1. Softmax also exaggerates the gaps: a slightly higher match becomes a clearly higher weight, so the strongest match stands out.

This is the real formula from the transformer paper: attention weights are softmax of the Query-Key match, scaled by the square root of the key size.

Try it in the figure. Click "it" and watch where its attention goes. Its strongest weight lands on "animal," which is what "it" refers to in the sentence. The model has no grammar rules telling it this. It falls out of the Query and Key vectors and the softmax over their matches.

The attention weights here are the real computation: a Query-Key dot product, scaled by the square root of the vector size, then softmax, exactly the transformer paper's formula. What is hand-authored is the Query, Key, and Value vectors themselves. A real model learns those from data over billions of words; here they are set by hand so the example is small enough to read. The math on top of them is genuine.
